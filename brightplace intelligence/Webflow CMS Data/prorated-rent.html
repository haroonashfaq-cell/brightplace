What Is Prorated Rent?

What is prorated rent? Prorated rent is a partial rent payment calculated based on the number of days a tenant occupies a rental unit in a given month, rather than the full monthly amount. The prorated rent meaning is straightforward: you pay only for the days you actually live in the apartment, not the full calendar month.

The term comes from the Latin "pro rata," meaning in proportion. It applies when you move in or move out on any day other than the first or last of the month. If you are moving into an apartment mid-month and have seen this term in your lease, here is what it means for your actual move-in costs: a renter moving in on the 16th of a 30-day month with $1,800/month rent owes $900 for that month.

How to Calculate Prorated Rent

The most common method for residential leases is the calendar days method. It uses the actual number of days in the month you move in.

The formula: Monthly rent / days in the month x days you occupy the unit = prorated rent.

Step-by-step calculation example

Suppose your monthly rent is $1,800 and you move in on June 16. June has 30 days.

  1. Identify your occupancy days. The move-in day itself counts as day one of occupancy. June 16 through June 30 = 15 days.
  2. Calculate your daily rent rate. The daily rent rate is the monthly rent divided by the number of days in the month. $1,800 / 30 = $60.00 per day.
  3. Multiply daily rate by occupancy days. $60.00 x 15 = $900.00.

You owe $900.00 in prorated rent for June. Your first full month of rent ($1,800) begins July 1.

The three methods landlords use

Not all landlords use the same calculation. Three methods exist. The dollar difference between them is real.

Calendar Days (Actual Month): Monthly rent / actual days in the month x occupancy days. Using $1,800 rent, June 16 move-in (15 days in a 30-day month): $1,800 / 30 x 15 = $900.00.

Banker's Month (30-Day Standard): Monthly rent / 30 x occupancy days, regardless of the actual month length. A banker's month is a standardized 30-day period some landlords use, applied regardless of the actual number of days in the calendar month. Using the same example: $1,800 / 30 x 15 = $900.00.

Annual Daily Rate (365-Day): Monthly rent x 12 / 365 x occupancy days. $1,800 x 12 / 365 x 15 = $887.67.

The calendar days method is the most common for residential leases. The banker's month produces the same result in 30-day months but differs in months with 28, 29, or 31 days. In a 31-day month, the same move-in date produces a different result under the banker's month method versus the calendar days method, with the banker's month producing a daily rate of $60.00 versus $58.06. Ask your landlord which method they use before signing.

When Prorated Rent Applies

Prorated rent applies in three primary scenarios.

Mid-month move-in. You sign a lease with a start date that falls after the 1st. Your landlord charges you only for the days remaining in that month. This is the most common scenario.

Mid-month move-out. Your lease ends before the last day of the month. You owe rent only through your last day of occupancy. Occupancy days are the number of days a tenant has possession of the unit in a partial month, counted from the move-in date (which itself counts as day one) through the last day of the month.

Early lease termination. If you break your lease and vacate mid-month, prorated rent may apply to the final partial month. The lease governs whether this is an option or whether you owe the full month regardless.

What Prorated Rent Does Not Cover

Prorated rent applies only to base rent. Several other move-in costs are not prorated, and confusing them is common.

Security deposits are not prorated. The full deposit amount is typically due at lease signing regardless of the move-in date. A security deposit is money paid upfront to the landlord, held against damage or unpaid rent and returned at move-out if the unit is left in good condition.

Utility setup fees are one-time charges, not prorated. Parking fees, administrative fees, and application fees are also typically due in full at signing. For a complete breakdown of what renters pay beyond base rent, see brightplace's guide to your true monthly cost.

What to Ask Your Landlord Before Signing

Three questions protect you from surprises on the move-in cost breakdown.

  1. Does the landlord prorate rent at all? Not all do. Some require full-month payment regardless of move-in date. Ask before assuming.
  2. Which calculation method do they use? Calendar days, banker's month, or annual daily rate. The method affects your actual bill.
  3. Is the prorated amount itemized separately? Your move-in cost breakdown should show prorated rent, security deposit, and any fees as separate line items. If the total is lumped together, ask for an itemized statement.

State laws on proration vary. New York requires proration by law under NY Real Property Law SS 232-a (required for rent-stabilized units, customary for market-rate). Massachusetts requires proration under General Laws Chapter 186, Section 4. California requires proration under Civil Code SS 1946. Texas and Pennsylvania do not mandate proration, but courts require "reasonable and proportionate" calculations. Most property management software in 2026 (TenantCloud, Buildium, Avail, and others) auto-calculates proration, making manual calculation less common for professionally managed properties.

Is Prorated Rent Required by Law?

Federal law does not require landlords to prorate rent in most states. The lease agreement governs whether proration applies. Some states, including California, Florida, Arizona, and states that have adopted the Uniform Residential Landlord and Tenant Act (URLTA), have statutes requiring that rent be apportionable on a daily basis by default.

If your lease does not address prorated rent, request a written addendum before signing. Do not assume proration is automatic. Local landlord-tenant laws vary, and the lease is the document that controls your obligations.

Frequently Asked Questions About Prorated Rent

What is prorated rent?

Prorated rent is a partial month's rent calculated by dividing your monthly rent by the number of days in the month, then multiplying by the days you occupy the unit. It applies when you move in or out on a day other than the first or last of the month, so you pay only for the days you actually live there rather than the full monthly amount.

What is an example of prorated rent?

If your monthly rent is $1,800 and you move in on June 16 in a 30-day month, you occupy the unit for 15 days. Your daily rate is $60 ($1,800 / 30). Your prorated rent is $900 ($60 x 15). Your first full rent payment of $1,800 begins July 1.

Is prorated rent good or bad for renters?

Prorated rent benefits renters because it means you pay only for the days you actually occupy the unit. Without proration, a mid-month move-in would require paying the full month's rent for partial occupancy. If a landlord does not prorate, negotiate a move-in date on the 1st to avoid overpaying.

What does prorated rent mean when renewing a lease?

Prorated rent at lease renewal applies when the new lease starts on a different date than the old one ended. For example, if your previous lease ends June 15 and your renewal starts June 16, the landlord may prorate June's rent for the 15 remaining days under the new lease terms.

When exactly is prorated rent due?

Prorated rent is typically due at move-in, alongside the security deposit and first month's rent. Some landlords collect the prorated amount as a separate payment; others include it in the move-in total. Ask for an itemized breakdown so you know exactly what each payment covers.

Does prorated rent affect my security deposit?

No. Security deposits are not prorated. The full deposit amount, typically equal to one month's rent, is due at lease signing regardless of when you move in during the month. Prorated rent applies only to the base rent for the partial occupancy period.

What if my landlord does not offer prorated rent?

If your landlord requires full-month payment regardless of move-in date, you have two options: negotiate a move-in date on the 1st of the month so you receive the full month you are paying for, or request a written addendum adding a proration clause to the lease before signing.
