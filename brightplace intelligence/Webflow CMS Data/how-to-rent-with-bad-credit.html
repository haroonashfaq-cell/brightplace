Renting with bad credit is harder, but it is not a dead end. Roughly 28% of U.S. consumers have a FICO score below the "Good" threshold of 670 (as of Q3 2026), and many of them sign leases every year. The difference between approval and rejection usually comes down to how you structure your application, not just the number on your credit report. This guide covers what landlords actually evaluate, which strategies increase your odds, and where to focus your search.

Can You Actually Get Approved for a Rental with Bad Credit?

Yes. A credit score below 620 makes the process harder, but landlords weigh multiple factors when reviewing an application. Your score is one data point. Income, rental history, and how you present your situation all carry weight. Most landlords use a FICO score as a screening tool, where 620 or above is the general preference (as of Q3 2026). A score in the 500s does not automatically disqualify you, especially with private landlords who set their own standards.

What Do Landlords Actually Look At Beyond Your Credit Score?

Landlords evaluate four factors when deciding whether to approve your rental application. Your credit score matters, but it is rarely the only reason for rejection. Understanding the full picture helps you strengthen the parts of your application you can control.

Credit score and payment history: Most landlords prefer a FICO score of 620 or higher (as of Q3 2026). They look at late payments, collections, and charge-offs. Medical debt in collections carries less weight than missed rent payments for most screeners.

Income relative to rent: The rent-to-income ratio is the landlord's standard check that your gross monthly income is at least three times the monthly rent. If your monthly rent is $1,200, you need to show at least $3,600 in gross monthly income (as of Q3 2026). Strong income often matters more than the credit score itself.

Rental history and references: A track record of on-time payments and clean lease completions can offset a low score. Written references from previous landlords confirming you paid on time and left the unit in good condition carry real weight. Outstanding debts and collections: Landlords check for active collections and judgments. A letter of explanation is a short written statement you provide with your rental application that describes what caused your credit issues and what has changed since. Prepare one before you apply.

What Are the Most Effective Strategies for Renting with Bad Credit?

Six specific approaches improve your chances of getting approved when your credit score is below 620. Start with the strategies that require the least effort and build from there.

1. Target private landlords instead of corporate complexes. Individual owners managing one to four units typically set their own screening criteria. They are more likely to consider your full situation rather than running your application through an automated system that rejects anything below a threshold. Look for rental postings by individuals rather than by property management companies. For more on apartments with no credit check, brightplace has a detailed guide.

2. Offer a larger security deposit. Putting down two to three months' rent reduces the landlord's risk. Check your state's deposit cap before making the offer. California caps security deposits at one month's rent for most units (as of Q3 2026, under AB 12). Many other states have no cap, meaning you can offer more to sweeten the deal.

3. Show proof of income before they ask. Bring three to six months of pay stubs, bank statements, or an employment letter showing income of at least three times the monthly rent. If you are self-employed, bring tax returns or 1099 forms from the most recent filing year. Landlords who see strong, documented income often approve applicants with lower credit scores. Check brightplace's rent affordability calculator to understand what you can comfortably afford.

4. Write a letter of explanation. Keep it to one page. State what happened (medical bills, job loss, divorce), what has changed (new job, payment plan, settled debts), and why it will not happen again. Landlords are human. A clear, honest explanation goes further than you might expect. The tradeoff: this only works with landlords who actually read applications manually, not automated corporate screeners.

5. Bring a co-signer or use a guarantor service. A co-signer is a person who signs the lease alongside you and agrees to pay your rent if you cannot. A guarantor is similar but typically does not live in the unit. Commercial guarantor services charge 4% to 10% of the annual rent (as of Q3 2026). A co-signer strengthens your application significantly, but it puts real financial responsibility on another person. Only ask someone who understands what they are agreeing to.

6. Apply to properties with longer vacancies. A unit that has been sitting empty for 30 or more days costs the landlord money. Landlords with zero applicants are more flexible than landlords with five. Check how long a unit has been posted. Older postings often mean more negotiating room.

How Can You Tell Which Landlords Are More Flexible?

Not every landlord screens the same way. Certain indicators in a rental posting suggest the owner is more open to working with applicants who have credit challenges. This is practical knowledge that most guides skip entirely.

Postings that include a personal phone number rather than a management company number usually point to an individual owner. Shorter, less polished descriptions often indicate a hands-on landlord who manages the property directly. The absence of phrases like "credit check required" or "minimum score of 650" in the posting is also telling.

Building size matters. A four-unit building typically has more flexible screening than a 200-unit complex managed by a corporate company with automated systems. Smaller buildings mean the owner makes the decision, not a software platform.

Long-vacant properties create negotiating room. A landlord who has had an empty unit for 45 days is losing $1,500 or more per month in potential rent (as of Q3 2026). That financial pressure creates room for a conversation about your qualifications beyond your credit score.

What Should You Do If You Have Bad Credit and Low Income?

The combination of bad credit and low income narrows your options, but specific programs exist for this situation. Applying for income-restricted housing is one path forward.

HUD's rental assistance programs use income eligibility as the primary qualifier rather than credit scores. Housing Choice Vouchers (Section 8) can cover a portion of your rent, and the credit screening for voucher holders is often less strict than market-rate rentals.

Private landlords in lower-cost markets sometimes apply different income standards than the 3x rule. In areas where rents are below $800 per month (as of Q3 2026), some landlords accept 2x income verification, especially when the applicant has steady employment.

A roommate arrangement sidesteps the individual credit issue. The primary leaseholder's credit is what matters, and you contribute your share of rent under a separate agreement. See brightplace's guide on renters insurance with roommates for shared-living protections.

How Should You Handle Bad Credit Combined with a Prior Eviction?

An eviction on your record alongside bad credit makes the search significantly harder. Eviction records typically appear on tenant screening reports for seven years and are separate from credit reports. Acknowledging this upfront is more effective than hoping it goes unnoticed.

Write a specific explanation: what happened, what changed, and why the situation will not repeat. Generic apologies do not work. A detailed account showing stable employment and financial changes since the eviction carries more weight.

Focus on private landlords in suburban areas and older Class B or C buildings where tenant demand is lower. These owners often screen individually rather than through automated rejection systems. Offering a larger deposit alongside your explanation strengthens your position.

Commercial guarantor services serve as a professional co-signer when no personal guarantor is available. The cost (4% to 10% of annual rent as of Q3 2026) is significant, but it can be the difference between approval and another rejection. How Can You Build Credit While Renting?

Building credit while you rent turns a difficult situation now into a stronger application next time. Rent-reporting services allow you to report monthly payments to one or more credit bureaus, building payment history even if your landlord does not report directly. Most charge $5 to $10 per month (as of Q3 2026).

Keep credit card balances below 30% of your available limit. This single factor accounts for roughly 30% of your FICO score (as of Q3 2026). Setting up autopay on existing accounts prevents further missed payments from dragging your score down.

Verify that your payments are appearing on your report through AnnualCreditReport.com after two to three months of reporting. Twelve months of consistent on-time rent payments can add 20 to 40 points to a thin credit file.

Frequently Asked Questions About Renting with Bad Credit

Can I rent an apartment with a 500 credit score?

Yes, though your options at large corporate-managed complexes will be limited. Private landlords who manage one to four units are the most realistic path. Offering a larger deposit, showing strong income, and providing landlord references can offset a score in the 500s. Some income-based programs do not use credit score thresholds at all.

What is the lowest credit score a landlord will accept?

Most large apartment complexes set a minimum between 620 and 670 (as of Q3 2026). Private landlords often have no formal minimum. Their decisions are based on income, rental history, and the overall application rather than a single number. Luxury properties may require 700 or higher.

Can I rent an apartment with bad credit but good income?

Strong income is often the deciding factor. If your gross monthly income is three times the monthly rent or higher, many landlords will approve your application despite a low credit score. Documenting your income with pay stubs, bank statements, and an employment letter strengthens this approach significantly.

How do I rent with bad credit and no co-signer?

Focus on private landlords, offer a larger security deposit, and prepare a letter explaining your credit history. Commercial guarantor services can also fill the co-signer role for a fee of 4% to 10% of your annual rent (as of Q3 2026). Showing proof of income at three times the rent is critical in this scenario.

Do landlords care more about credit score or income?

Most landlords prioritize income over credit score. A renter earning $5,000 per month with a 550 credit score is often more appealing than someone earning $2,800 with a 680 score and the same rent target. Income proves current ability to pay. A credit score reflects past behavior, which can change.

Can I find an apartment with bad credit in California?

California's tenant protections under AB 1482 limit annual rent increases to 5% plus CPI for covered properties (as of Q3 2026). Security deposits are capped at one month's rent under AB 12. These caps mean landlords cannot charge extra to offset risk, which makes income proof and references even more important for California applicants.

Does applying for an apartment hurt my credit score?

It depends on the type of inquiry. A soft pull, which many landlords and screening services use, does not affect your score. A hard pull can lower your score by two to five points and stays on your credit report for two years. Ask the landlord which type of check they run before consenting.

What is a letter of explanation for a rental application?

A letter of explanation is a one-page document submitted with your rental application that describes what caused your credit issues, what has changed since, and why the landlord can expect reliable payments going forward. Focus on facts: dates, amounts, employment changes. Keep the tone professional and direct.

Are second-chance apartments real?

Yes. Some apartment communities specifically market to renters with past evictions, broken leases, or low credit scores. They often require a larger deposit and may charge higher rent. The tradeoff is access to housing when other options are exhausted. These are most common in larger metro areas with higher vacancy rates.

How long does bad credit affect my ability to rent?

Negative items like late payments and collections stay on your credit report for up to seven years under the Fair Credit Reporting Act. The impact lessens over time, especially if you build positive payment history. After two to three years of on-time payments, most landlords weigh recent behavior more heavily than older marks.
