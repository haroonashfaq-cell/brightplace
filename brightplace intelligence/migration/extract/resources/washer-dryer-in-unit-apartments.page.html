What Does In-Unit Washer and Dryer Mean?

In-unit washer and dryer means the machines are physically inside your apartment, provided and maintained by the property management company. This is different from hookups, where the water supply lines and electrical outlets exist but you buy or rent your own machines. A third option, shared laundry, means coin-operated or card-based machines in a common room.

About 70% of new Class A apartment construction since 2020 includes in-unit laundry as standard (as of Q3 2026). That percentage drops sharply in older buildings, particularly in cities like San Francisco, New York, and Chicago where pre-war and mid-century housing stock dominates.

Stackable units are the most common configuration in apartments. They measure 24 to 27 inches wide and fit inside a closet or alcove. Side-by-side full-size units (27 to 30 inches wide each) require a dedicated laundry room, which newer builds increasingly include. The key distinction for renters: "in-unit" means the property provides the appliances. "Hookups" means you provide them yourself. Listings are not always clear about this, so confirm during your tour. Many renters have signed leases expecting machines and found empty hookups instead.

How Much More Is Rent with a Washer and Dryer in Unit?

Apartments with washer and dryer in unit typically cost $50 to $100 more per month than comparable units without laundry (as of Q3 2026). The exact premium depends on the city, building age, and whether in-unit laundry is standard in the local market or a retrofit.

Here is how the rent premium breaks down by city:

  • National average: $50 to $100/mo extra (as of Q3 2026). In a survey of 172,703 renters, the average amount renters said they would pay for in-unit laundry was $54.73/mo.
  • Dallas, Houston, Atlanta: $30 to $75/mo premium (as of Q3 2026). Sun Belt cities with newer construction stock have higher supply of in-unit laundry, which keeps premiums low.
  • Chicago: Approximately $73/mo premium (as of Q3 2026). Many vintage buildings in neighborhoods like Logan Square and Wicker Park lack hookups entirely.
  • Philadelphia: Approximately 20% premium, roughly $211/mo extra (as of Q3 2026). Older rowhome conversions rarely include laundry infrastructure.
  • Manhattan: $578/mo average premium for a 1BR, with in-unit units averaging $4,984/mo versus $4,406/mo without (as of Q3 2026). This is the highest premium in the country.

The pattern is consistent. Cities with newer housing stock (Texas, Florida, Georgia, Arizona) charge smaller premiums because in-unit laundry is standard. Cities with older stock (New York, Philadelphia, San Francisco) charge significantly more because adding laundry requires plumbing and venting retrofits. When budgeting, factor this premium into what percentage of income should go to rent to make sure the upgrade fits your finances.

What Is the Difference Between Washer Dryer Hookups and In-Unit?

Hookups and in-unit are not the same thing, and confusing the two is the most common mistake renters make when searching for apartments with in-unit laundry. Here is how the four laundry options compare, including a 12-month cost breakdown.

  • In-unit (provided): Machines included in rent and maintained by the property. No upfront cost. Premium: $50 to $100/mo (as of Q3 2026). 12-month cost: $600 to $1,200 in additional rent. Downside: compact machines (2.0 to 2.5 cu ft capacity) may not handle bulky items like comforters.
  • Hookups only: Water lines and electrical outlets are installed, but you supply the machines. Upfront cost: $800 to $1,200 to buy a basic set, or $30 to $50/mo to rent appliances (as of Q3 2026). 12-month cost (if renting machines): $360 to $600. 12-month cost (if buying): $800 to $1,200 one-time, then $0. Upside: you choose the brand, capacity, and features.
  • Shared laundry room: Community machines on-site, typically coin-operated at $3 to $5 per load (as of Q3 2026). 12-month cost: $144 to $480 in machine fees, plus 24 to 48 hours per year of your time hauling, waiting, and watching cycles.
  • Laundromat (off-site): $3 to $6 per load plus travel time (as of Q3 2026). Average renter spends $50 to $100/mo. 12-month cost: $600 to $1,200 in fees, plus time, gas, and the inconvenience of leaving home.

The bottom line: in-unit laundry and laundromat visits cost roughly the same in dollars over 12 months. The real difference is time. In-unit saves 24 to 48 hours per year that you would otherwise spend hauling clothes and waiting for machines. If hookups are available and the premium exceeds $150/mo, buying your own machines pays for itself in six to eight months. Are In-Unit Washers and Dryers Worth the Extra Rent?

For most renters, in-unit laundry is worth the premium up to about $100 per month (as of Q3 2026). Beyond that threshold, the math tilts toward buying your own machines for hookups or using shared laundry. But cost alone does not capture the full picture.

The time savings matter. Renters with shared laundry or laundromat routines spend two to four hours per month on laundry logistics: carrying baskets, waiting for open machines, making return trips. If you value your time at even $15/hr, that is $30 to $60/mo in time cost on top of the machine fees.

There is also the convenience factor that numbers cannot capture. Doing laundry at 11 PM without leaving your apartment, washing a single item on short notice, and never worrying about someone moving your wet clothes out of a shared machine. Renters who switch to in-unit laundry consistently describe it as a permanent lifestyle upgrade.

Honest tradeoffs to weigh: apartment-size stackable washer dryers typically have 2.0 to 2.5 cubic feet of capacity versus 4.5+ for full-size machines. Bulky loads like king-size comforters or heavy blankets may still require a trip to a laundromat. In-unit machines can also add $30 to $50/mo to your water and electric bill (as of Q3 2026), a cost renters often overlook when comparing the rent premium alone. Make sure to check whether utilities are included or metered separately. If you are looking for move-in specials, some properties offer the first month at a reduced rate that offsets the laundry premium.

What Should You Ask About Laundry Before Signing a Lease?

The right questions during an apartment tour can prevent surprises after move-in. brightplace recommends confirming these details before signing, especially if in-unit laundry is a priority.

  • Are the washer and dryer included in rent, or is there a separate monthly fee? Some properties charge a $25 to $50/mo amenity fee (as of Q3 2026) on top of rent for in-unit appliance access.
  • What is the machine capacity? Compact stackable units (2.0 to 2.5 cu ft) handle everyday loads but struggle with bulky bedding. Full-size units (4.5+ cu ft) handle everything.
  • Who handles maintenance and repairs? If machines are property-provided, maintenance is typically the landlord's responsibility. Confirm this in writing.
  • If hookups only, what connections are provided? You need to know whether the dryer outlet is 120V or 240V, and whether it is gas or electric. Buying the wrong dryer for your hookup is an expensive mistake.
  • Is there a shared laundry room as backup? Useful if your in-unit machine breaks down.
  • Are there restrictions on running machines during certain hours? Some buildings limit evening or early morning use due to noise.
  • How are water and sewer billed? In-unit machines add $30 to $50/mo to utility bills (as of Q3 2026). Know whether water is included in rent or metered separately.

Can You Install a Washer and Dryer in an Apartment Without Hookups?

Portable and ventless appliances let renters add laundry capability even in apartments without hookups, but each option comes with real limitations. Always check your lease before purchasing or installing any appliance.

  • Portable washing machines: Connect to a kitchen or bathroom sink via an adapter. Cost: $200 to $500 (as of Q3 2026). Capacity: roughly one-third of a standard machine. Load times are longer and cleaning performance is noticeably weaker on heavy fabrics.
  • Ventless condenser dryers: Run without an external vent by condensing moisture internally. Cost: $500 to $900 (as of Q3 2026). They work, but drying cycles take two to three times longer than vented machines and clothes sometimes come out damp.
  • Combo washer-dryer units: A single machine that washes and dries. Cost: $800 to $1,500 (as of Q3 2026). Convenient in theory, but cycles take four to five hours per load and drying performance is consistently poor based on renter feedback.
  • Countertop spin dryers: A compact device that removes excess water before air-drying. Cost: $50 to $150 (as of Q3 2026). Not a true dryer, but useful as a supplement.

The honest assessment: portable machines are a stopgap, not a replacement for real in-unit laundry. They work for a single person doing small loads, but couples and families will find the capacity and performance frustrating. Some landlords also prohibit portable machines due to water damage risk. Confirm in writing before buying. Consider renters insurance to protect your belongings in case of leaks, and review your state's tenant rights through the CFPB renter resources page for more guidance on appliance modifications.
