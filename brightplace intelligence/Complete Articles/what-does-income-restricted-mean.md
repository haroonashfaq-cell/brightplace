# What Income Restricted Means for Apartment Renters

*Last reviewed: August 2026*

Income restricted means a rental unit has a legal cap on both who can move in and what the landlord can charge, tied to the Area Median Income (AMI) for that location. Most income restricted apartments are designated for households earning 60% or less of AMI, the threshold used by the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program. The rent ceiling is set by the unit's designation, not by what the renter earns. HUD published FY 2026 income limits on May 1, 2026, with limits rising an average of 3.4% nationally, though increases were capped at 10% (as of Q3 2026).

If you have seen "income restricted" on a listing and are not sure whether you qualify, this guide covers how eligibility works, the step-by-step qualification process, how rent is calculated, and what actually happens if your income changes after you move in.

## How Does Area Median Income (AMI) Determine Eligibility?

Area Median Income is the household income at the midpoint of the income distribution for a specific metro area, recalculated annually by [HUD](https://www.hud.gov/topics/rental_assistance). Your eligibility for an income restricted apartment depends on which AMI tier the unit is designated for and your household size. HUD publishes updated income limits each year through the [HUD USER Income Limits portal](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/il.html), and the FY 2026 figures became effective May 1, 2026.

The most common tier is 60% AMI, because LIHTC tax-credit properties, which make up the largest share of income restricted apartments in the United States, typically use this threshold. LIHTC properties use the Multifamily Tax Subsidy Project (MTSP) income limits, published separately by HUD, which can differ slightly from standard Section 8 limits in some areas.

Here is how the four main AMI tiers work:

**30% AMI (Extremely Low Income):** Targets households with the lowest incomes. Most common programs: public housing and Housing Choice Vouchers. In New York City, the FY 2026 limit for a single person at 30% AMI is approximately $29,100 (as of Q3 2026).

**50% AMI (Very Low Income):** Covers voucher eligibility, Section 202 senior housing, and Section 811 disability housing. In New York City, a single person at 50% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $48,500 (as of Q3 2026).

**60% AMI (Low to Moderate Income):** The standard tier for LIHTC tax-credit apartments. This is the tier renters encounter most often when a listing says "income restricted." In New York City, a four-person household at 60% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $83,130 (as of Q3 2026).

**80% AMI (Moderate Income):** Used in some public housing and HOME-funded developments. In New York City, a single person at 80% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $77,600 (as of Q3 2026). In lower-cost metros, 80% AMI restricted rents may not offer much savings over market rate.

HUD recalculates AMI limits every year, and figures for a specific county can rise or fall. For FY 2026, roughly one in six areas saw limits decrease compared to 2025, so last year's eligibility determination may not apply this year. Always check the current limits for your specific county through [HUD's income limits tool](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/il.html) before applying.

## How Do You Qualify for Income-Restricted Housing in 2026?

Qualifying for income restricted housing follows a structured process, but the paperwork load is heavier than a standard market-rate application. Here is the step-by-step process, based on current [HUD program requirements](https://www.hud.gov/hud-partners/hotma) and LIHTC compliance rules.

**Step 1: Determine your household size and gross income.** Count every person who will live in the unit. Add gross annual income from all adults, including wages, self-employment income, Social Security, pensions, child support, alimony, and income from assets. The total is annualized and projected forward, not averaged from past earnings.

**Step 2: Look up the AMI limits for your county.** Use HUD's [income limits portal](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/il.html) to find the current limits for your metro area by household size. LIHTC properties specifically use the [MTSP income limits](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/mtsp.html), which may differ from standard Section 8 limits.

**Step 3: Check the HOTMA asset limit.** Under rules updated by the Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act ([HOTMA](https://www.hud.gov/hud-partners/hotma)), HUD-assisted programs now impose a net family asset limit of $105,574 for 2026, adjusted annually for inflation. This applies to public housing and Section 8 programs. LIHTC properties may have separate asset policies set by their state housing finance agency.

**Step 4: Gather your documentation.** Properties will ask for tax returns (most recent year), consecutive pay stubs (typically 30 to 60 days), benefit award letters, bank and asset statements, and a signed authorization to verify income with employers and agencies. Having these ready before you apply prevents delays that can cost you a spot on a waitlist.

**Step 5: Apply to multiple properties simultaneously.** No single national database lists every available income restricted unit. Start with [HUD's Resource Locator](https://www.hud.gov/topics/rental_assistance), your [state housing finance agency's](https://www.ncsha.org/housing-finance-agencies/) LIHTC property list, and your local Public Housing Authority (PHA). Waitlists open and close on unpredictable schedules, and a missed notification can cost you your place.

**Step 6: Complete income certification.** Once a unit becomes available, the property manager will conduct a formal income certification, verifying every source of income and comparing it against the unit's AMI tier. This process typically takes two to four weeks. You must be income-eligible at the time of certification, not at the time you joined the waitlist.

A common frustration renters report: passing the income check only to be screened out by a separate tenant screening for credit, rental history, or background. Income eligibility and tenant screening are two separate gates. For more on how credit affects your application, see brightplace's guide to [apartments with no credit check](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/apartments-with-no-credit-check).

## What Are 2026 AMI Income Limits for Major Cities?

AMI limits vary dramatically by metro area, because they reflect local incomes, not a national average. Below are approximate FY 2026 income limits at the 60% AMI tier, the most common threshold for income restricted LIHTC apartments. All figures are based on HUD FY 2026 data published May 1, 2026 (as of Q3 2026).

**New York City (NYC metro area):** A four-person household at 60% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $83,130. A single person at 60% AMI: approximately $58,190. NYC also uses higher AMI bands (80%, 100%, 120%, and up to 165% AMI) for city-funded affordable housing lotteries, meaning some "income restricted" listings in NYC have higher ceilings than LIHTC properties in other cities.

**Houston (Houston-The Woodlands-Sugar Land metro area):** A four-person household at 50% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $47,350. At 60% AMI, that figure rises to approximately $56,820 (as of Q3 2026).

**Minneapolis-St. Paul metro area:** A single person at 50% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $46,350. At 60% AMI, the single-person limit is approximately $55,620 (as of Q3 2026).

The dollar amounts shift every year when HUD publishes new limits. LIHTC properties benefit from a "hold harmless" provision: they can use the highest income limits published since the property was placed in service, protecting both residents and operators from year-over-year drops in AMI. All LIHTC properties must begin using the new 2026 income and rent limits by June 15, 2026 (as of Q3 2026, per [HUD compliance guidance](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/mtsp.html)).

## What Is the Difference Between Income Restricted and Income Based Housing?

The core difference is who controls the rent amount. In an income restricted apartment, rent is capped at a fixed amount tied to the unit's AMI tier. In income based housing, rent is calculated as roughly 30% of your own adjusted monthly income, so it rises and falls as your paycheck changes.

**Income Restricted (how rent is set):** Fixed cap tied to the unit's AMI tier. Rent does not change if your income rises or drops. Eligibility is monitored by the property and its auditor. Examples: LIHTC apartments, local affordability set-asides.

**Income Based (how rent is set):** Roughly 30% of your adjusted income. Rent increases if your income rises, decreases if it drops. Eligibility is monitored per-tenant by the housing authority. Examples: Section 8 Housing Choice Vouchers, public housing.

The practical test is one question: ask the leasing office, "Does my rent change if my income changes?" If the answer is no, the unit is income restricted. If yes, it is [income based](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/income-based-homes-charlotte-nc). This distinction also answers a common question about whether income restricted means Section 8. It does not. The **Section 8 Housing Choice Voucher program** is a specific federal voucher program where a housing authority pays a portion of rent in private apartments. Income restricted is a broader category that includes LIHTC, public housing, project-based assistance, and local set-asides. Vouchers can be used in some income restricted buildings, but the two are separate programs.

## What Is the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) Program?

The **Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC)** is a federal program created by the Tax Reform Act of 1986 that gives tax credits to private developers in exchange for reserving a portion of their units for income-qualified renters at capped rents. LIHTC is the single largest source of income restricted apartments in the United States, responsible for financing approximately 3.6 million units since its creation (as of Q3 2026, per [National Council of State Housing Agencies](https://www.ncsha.org/housing-finance-agencies/) estimates).

Each state receives an annual allocation of tax credits. For 2026, the IRS set the per-capita LIHTC ceiling at $2.90, multiplied by the state's population (as of Q3 2026). State housing finance agencies award these credits to developers through a competitive application process, and in return, the developer must keep designated units income restricted for a minimum of 30 years (initial 15-year compliance period plus a 15-year extended use agreement, per IRS program requirements).

LIHTC properties come in two types that affect who can apply:

**100% Affordable Properties:** Every unit in the building is income restricted. These properties have been exempt from full annual income recertification since 2008, under the Housing and Economic Recovery Act (HERA). Tenants complete an annual self-certification instead.

**Mixed-Income Properties:** Some units are market-rate, others are income restricted. The income restricted units follow the same AMI rules, but the building may also offer unrestricted units at higher rents.

Student households face a specific LIHTC rule: if every member of the household is a full-time student, the household is generally ineligible unless an exception applies (single parents, veterans, married couples eligible to file jointly, or certain job training participants).

## How Is Rent Calculated in Income Restricted Apartments?

In a LIHTC tax-credit unit, rent is not based on what you personally earn. Rent is capped at 30% of an imputed income for 1.5 persons per bedroom at the unit's designated AMI tier (standard LIHTC rent formula, per [HUD MTSP tables](https://www.huduser.gov/portal/datasets/mtsp.html)).

Here is a worked example. A two-bedroom unit assumes a three-person household (1.5 persons per bedroom). If the three-person income limit at 60% AMI in a given metro area is approximately $75,660 (as of Q3 2026), the gross rent ceiling is $75,660 x 30% / 12 = approximately $1,891 per month before any utility allowance reduction. When tenants pay utilities directly, the property subtracts a utility allowance from that cap, lowering the effective maximum rent.

This formula means a single person renting a two-bedroom LIHTC unit faces the same rent cap as any other qualified renter. The cap follows the unit, not the tenant. This is the opposite of income based programs like Section 8, where you pay 30% of your own adjusted income. For a broader look at how rent costs add up, see brightplace's guide to [your true monthly cost](https://www.brightplace.ai/guides/your-true-monthly-cost).

One detail that catches renters off guard: LIHTC properties also set minimum income requirements, typically 2 to 2.5 times the monthly rent. A renter earning too little to cover even the reduced rent will be screened out. This minimum is often waived for Housing Choice Voucher holders, since the voucher covers a portion of rent.

## What Happens If Your Income Goes Up in an Income Restricted Apartment?

In a LIHTC tax-credit unit, a raise does not force you out and does not increase your rent. The rent ceiling moves only when HUD's published AMI limits change, not when your paycheck changes. Fully affordable LIHTC properties have been exempt from the annual third-party income recheck since 2008, when the Housing and Economic Recovery Act (HERA) eliminated that requirement for 100% LIHTC buildings. Tenants still complete an annual self-certification, but a full recertification with documentation is not required in these properties.

The 140% rule applies at the upper end: if your income rises above 140% of the qualifying limit, the owner must rent the next available comparable unit to a qualified household. You are not evicted. Your rent does not increase. The obligation shifts to the property, not to you.

Contrast this with Section 8 and public housing. In those programs, **annual recertification** requires you to verify income each year, and your rent is recalculated. A raise means higher rent. Earning above the program limit can eventually move you off the program entirely.

The practical takeaway: getting a promotion is safe in a capped-rent LIHTC unit. Budget for rent increases only if you are in an income based program. ## How Do You Find Income Restricted Apartments Near You?

No single national database lists every available income restricted unit. Finding them requires working multiple channels simultaneously.

The [National Council of State Housing Agencies](https://www.ncsha.org/housing-finance-agencies/) maintains a directory of every state agency that administers LIHTC credits. Contact your local Public Housing Authority (PHA) for voucher and public housing waitlists. Many PHAs post waitlist openings on their websites, but notification timing is inconsistent, which is why applying to multiple lists at the same time matters.

Gather documentation before you apply: tax returns, consecutive pay stubs (typically 30 to 60 days), benefit award letters, and asset statements. ## Frequently Asked Questions About Income Restricted Apartments

### Are income restricted apartments easier to get approved for?

Approval for income restricted apartments is more structured than market-rate rentals because the income cutoff is a clear threshold, not a landlord's subjective judgment. That said, the application process requires more documentation, including pay stubs, tax returns, and asset verification. Waitlists are common, especially in high-demand cities. The barrier is paperwork and patience, not complexity.

### Does income restricted mean Section 8?

No. Section 8 is one specific federal program, the Housing Choice Voucher program, where a housing authority pays a portion of rent to a private landlord. Income restricted is a broader category that includes LIHTC tax-credit apartments, public housing, project-based rental assistance, and local affordability set-asides. A Section 8 voucher can sometimes be used in an income restricted building, but the two terms are not interchangeable.

### What does 80 percent AMI income restricted mean?

A unit designated at 80% AMI is available to households earning up to 80% of the Area Median Income for their metro area and household size (as of Q3 2026). This is a moderate threshold. More people qualify for 80% AMI units than for 60% or 50% AMI units. In New York City, a single person at 80% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $77,600 (as of Q3 2026). In many metros, 80% AMI covers working professionals in fields like education, healthcare support, and food service.

### What does income restricted mean for a single person?

A single-person household has a lower AMI dollar ceiling than a multi-person household at the same percentage tier, because AMI tables scale by household size. The percentage threshold stays the same; the dollar amount is lower. In NYC, a single person at 60% AMI qualifies with income up to approximately $58,190 (as of Q3 2026), compared to approximately $83,130 for a household of four.

### Can you be removed from an income restricted apartment if your income increases?

In a LIHTC tax-credit unit, no. Your rent stays at the capped rate as long as you were eligible at move-in. If your income exceeds 140% of the qualifying limit, the owner must rent the next comparable unit to a qualified household, but you face no immediate eviction. In Section 8 and public housing, rising income increases your rent payment and can eventually move you off the program.

### What income is counted when applying for income restricted housing?

Income is counted broadly: wages, self-employment income, Social Security, pensions, child support, alimony, and income from assets all count. The total is annualized and projected forward, not averaged from past earnings. Scholarships applied to tuition are generally excluded. One-time payments like insurance settlements are often excluded. Omissions discovered later can void an approval, so disclose all sources upfront.

### Is there a minimum income to rent an income restricted apartment?

Yes. Most LIHTC properties set a minimum income requirement, separate from the AMI cap. Properties require evidence that the tenant can cover the reduced rent, typically 2 to 2.5 times the monthly rent in gross income (as of Q3 2026). This minimum is often waived for Housing Choice Voucher holders, since the voucher itself covers a portion of the rent.

### How long does it take to get into income restricted housing?

Wait times range from a few months at newer tax-credit buildings in lower-demand markets to several years at public housing or voucher waitlists in high-cost cities. No universal average applies. The most effective approach is applying to multiple waitlists simultaneously and keeping your contact information current with each agency, since a missed notification can cost you your place.

### What is the difference between income restricted and affordable housing?

Affordable housing is a broad umbrella term covering any housing priced below market rate, including subsidized units, rent-controlled apartments, and naturally occurring [cheap apartments](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/cheap-one-bedroom-apartments). Income restricted is a specific legal category within affordable housing where both tenant eligibility and rent are capped by AMI thresholds enforced through programs like LIHTC or local ordinances.

### Do income restricted apartments have waitlists?

Most income restricted apartments maintain waitlists, particularly in high-demand metro areas. LIHTC properties manage their own waitlists independently, which sometimes makes them shorter than centralized public housing lists. Waitlists open and close on unpredictable schedules. Applying to multiple properties and programs simultaneously is the most practical strategy for reducing wait time (as of Q3 2026).

### What is the HOTMA asset limit for income restricted housing?

Under the Housing Opportunity Through Modernization Act (HOTMA), HUD-assisted programs now impose a net family asset limit of $105,574 for 2026, adjusted annually for inflation (as of Q3 2026, per [HUD HOTMA guidance](https://www.hud.gov/hud-partners/hotma)). This limit applies to new applicants for public housing and Section 8 rental assistance. Assets include bank accounts, investments, and real property, but exclude personal belongings, necessary household goods, and certain retirement accounts. LIHTC-only properties may follow different asset rules set by their state housing finance agency.

### What documents do I need to apply for income restricted apartments?

Most income restricted properties require your most recent federal tax return, 30 to 60 days of consecutive pay stubs from all employed household members, Social Security or benefit award letters, bank statements for all accounts, and a signed authorization for the property to verify income directly with employers and agencies. Self-employed applicants should prepare a profit-and-loss statement and two years of tax returns. Having documents organized before you apply speeds the process and prevents losing a waitlist spot to delays.
