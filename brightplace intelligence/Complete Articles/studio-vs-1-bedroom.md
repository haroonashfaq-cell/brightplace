# What Renters Should Know About Studio vs. 1 Bedroom Apartments

A studio apartment combines living, sleeping, and kitchen space into one open room, with only the bathroom behind a door. A one-bedroom apartment separates the bedroom from the living area with walls and a closing door. Studios typically cost 15 to 30 percent less than one-bedroom units in the same building (as of Q3 2026), making the choice a tradeoff between monthly savings and daily comfort.

*Last reviewed: August 2026*

## What Is the Core Difference Between a Studio and a 1 Bedroom?

The difference between a studio and a 1 bedroom comes down to one wall and one door. A studio apartment is a single open room where you sleep, cook, eat, and relax in the same space. Only the bathroom is enclosed. A one-bedroom apartment adds a dedicated sleeping room separated by walls and a door, creating two distinct living zones.

That structural distinction changes daily life more than most renters expect. In a studio, everything shares one visual field. Your bed is visible when you open the front door. In a one-bedroom, you can close a door between your daytime activity and your sleeping space. This matters for [remote workers](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/cheap-one-bedroom-apartments), couples, and anyone who hosts guests.

An alcove studio falls between the two. This layout uses a recessed sleeping area created by the floor plan, offering partial visual separation without a full wall. Alcove studios typically rent for 5 to 10 percent more than standard studios in the same building (as of Q3 2026).

## How Does the Cost Actually Break Down Between Studio and 1 Bedroom?

Studios typically cost $300 to $1,100 less per month than one-bedroom apartments in the same market, though the gap varies significantly by city (as of Q3 2026). The rent delta, meaning the monthly dollar difference between studio and 1 bedroom rent in the same area, is the single most important number for budget-conscious renters.

Here is how the cost comparison looks across five major rental markets:

**New York City:** Average studio rent is approximately $3,800/mo; average 1BR rent is approximately $4,400/mo, a monthly difference of roughly $600 (as of Q3 2026).

**San Francisco:** Average studio rent is approximately $2,400/mo; average 1BR rent is approximately $3,500/mo, a monthly difference of roughly $800 (as of Q3 2026).

**Los Angeles:** Average studio rent is approximately $1,900/mo; average 1BR rent is approximately $2,300/mo, a monthly difference of roughly $700 (as of Q3 2026).

**Chicago:** Average studio rent is approximately $1,600/mo; average 1BR rent is approximately $2,300/mo, a monthly difference of roughly $700 (as of Q3 2026).

**Austin:** Average studio rent is approximately $1,300/mo; average 1BR rent is approximately $1,600/mo, a monthly difference of roughly $300 (as of Q3 2026).

These figures are directional estimates. Verify against current availability in your specific neighborhood.

Utility costs add another layer. Studios typically cost $50 to $100 per month less to heat and cool than a one-bedroom of similar vintage, simply because there is less space to condition (as of Q3 2026). Over a 12-month lease, that adds $600 to $1,200 in savings on top of the rent difference.

One important nuance: in dense urban markets, a studio in a prime location can cost more than a one-bedroom in a secondary neighborhood. Location can override unit type as a price factor. ## What Does a Studio Actually Feel Like Day to Day?

Living in a studio means your bed is always in view. Cooking smells reach your pillow. Visitors see your entire living space the moment they walk in. Your desk, your couch, and your kitchen counter compete for the same square footage, typically 400 to 600 square feet (as of Q3 2026).

For renters who spend most of their day outside the apartment, minimalists, or those on a tight first-year budget, a studio works well. The smaller footprint means less cleaning, less furniture to buy, and a lower monthly cost. Many first-time renters find studios practical precisely because they simplify everything. If you are [renting your first apartment](https://www.brightplace.ai/guides/how-to-rent-an-apartment), a studio keeps upfront costs manageable.

The friction shows up for renters who cook frequently, host friends, or need quiet focus time at home. Without a door to close, every activity shares the same room.

## What Does a 1 Bedroom Actually Feel Like Day to Day?

A closed bedroom door changes how an apartment feels more than an extra 200 square feet does. One-bedrooms typically range from 600 to 800 square feet (as of Q3 2026), but the real value is spatial separation. You can take a call in the living room while a partner sleeps. Guests can stay in the shared space without accessing the bedroom.

The tradeoffs are real. One-bedrooms cost more to rent, require more furniture to fill, and take longer to clean. Renters moving from a studio into a 1 bedroom often underestimate how much additional furnishing costs, sometimes $1,500 to $3,000 for a bedroom set and additional storage (as of Q3 2026). Understanding your [true monthly cost](https://www.brightplace.ai/guides/your-true-monthly-cost) helps avoid surprises.

For couples, the separate bedroom becomes essential once two daily routines overlap. Different sleep schedules, different work hours, or different noise preferences all benefit from a door that closes.

## How Does Remote Work Change the Studio vs. 1 Bedroom Calculation?

Remote work is the most important post-2020 variable in the studio vs 1 bedroom decision, and most comparison articles ignore it entirely. Renters who work from home 3 or more days per week consistently report greater difficulty maintaining work-life separation in a studio setting over a 12-month period.

The problem is cognitive, not spatial. In a studio, your visual field never changes. You finish work, close the laptop, and you are still staring at the same walls you worked against all day. In a one-bedroom, walking from a living room desk into a separate bedroom creates a physical transition that supports mental separation.

The practical recommendation: if you work from home full-time or on a hybrid schedule of 3 or more days per week, the one-bedroom pays for itself in sustained productivity and mental clarity. If you are fully in-office, the studio becomes the financially rational choice. Renters weighing a [short-term lease](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/short-term-lease-agreement) might test a studio for 3 to 6 months before committing to a longer one-bedroom lease.

## Which Layout Is Right for Your Situation?

The right choice depends on your daily routine, not your preferences on paper. Here are specific scenarios with direct recommendations.

If you earn under $45,000 per year and rent alone, a studio keeps you within the [30 percent of income guideline](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/rent-affordability-18-an-hour) in most mid-tier markets. The savings compound over a 12-month lease.

If you work from home 3 or more days per week, choose the one-bedroom. The cost premium pays for itself in sustained focus and reduced burnout over a full lease term.

If you are a couple sharing an apartment, the one-bedroom is almost always the right call. Two people in a studio means zero personal space during conflict, overlapping schedules, or sick days.

If you travel frequently and treat the apartment as a place to sleep, the studio makes financial sense. You are paying for less space you will rarely use fully.

If you host friends or family regularly, the one-bedroom lets you entertain in the living area without exposing the bedroom. Studios eliminate that separation entirely.

If you are signing your first lease and uncertain about your needs, consider a [month-to-month arrangement](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/month-to-month-vs-12-month-lease) in a studio to test the layout before committing long-term.

## What Do Most Renters Underestimate When Choosing Between Studio and 1 Bedroom?

Most renters focus on price and square footage but overlook design quality within the studio category. Not all studios perform the same. A well-designed 550-square-foot studio with 9-foot ceilings, two windows, and a sleeping alcove can feel more livable than a poorly designed 700-square-foot one-bedroom with a long hallway and limited natural light.

When touring a studio, evaluate these four variables that competitors rarely mention:

**Ceiling height:** Studios under 8 feet feel cramped regardless of square footage. Anything above 9 feet opens the space dramatically.

**Kitchen sight-line to the bed:** If you can see the stove from where you sleep, cooking smells and visual clutter will compound. Studios with L-shaped or galley kitchens that face away from the sleeping area perform better.

**Window count and direction:** South-facing or west-facing windows with unobstructed light make studios feel 20 to 30 percent larger than their square footage suggests. Studios with one north-facing window feel like a cave.

**Closet-to-square-footage ratio:** Studios under 500 square feet need at least one walk-in or reach-in closet to be practical. Without built-in storage, you lose livable floor space to wardrobes and shelving.

## Frequently Asked Questions About Studio vs. 1 Bedroom Apartments

### Is a studio or one bedroom better?

Neither is universally better. A studio suits solo renters on a budget who spend most of their time outside the apartment. A one-bedroom suits couples, remote workers, and anyone who needs spatial separation between activities. The right choice depends on your daily routine, work situation, and how much time you spend at home.

### What is cheaper, a 1 bedroom or studio?

Studios are almost always cheaper. They typically rent for 15 to 30 percent less than a one-bedroom in the same building or neighborhood (as of Q3 2026). In New York City, that gap can reach $1,100 per month. In mid-tier markets like Austin or Chicago, the difference runs $300 to $400 per month. Utility savings add another $20 to $50 monthly.

### What are the downsides of a studio apartment?

The main downsides are limited privacy, no separation between sleeping and living areas, cooking smells reaching the bed, and minimal storage space. Guests see your entire apartment upon entry. Remote workers often struggle with focus because the visual environment never changes. Studios under 450 square feet also limit furniture placement significantly.

### Can a couple live comfortably in a studio?

A couple can manage in a studio short-term, but comfort drops over a 12-month lease. Two people sharing one open room means overlapping schedules, no personal retreat space, and amplified noise from cooking, calls, and daily routines. Most couples find a one-bedroom essential by the six-month mark, especially if either partner works from home.

### Is a studio apartment worth it if I work from home?

For full-time remote workers, a studio creates a challenging environment after a few months. The inability to physically leave a workspace at the end of the day contributes to burnout. If you work from home 3 or more days per week, a one-bedroom is worth the extra cost. Fully in-office renters benefit from the studio cost savings.

### Why are some studio apartments more expensive than 1 bedrooms?

Location and building quality drive this. A studio in a prime downtown high-rise with amenities, high ceilings, and a doorman can cost more than a one-bedroom in a walkup building two miles away. Within the studio category, design variables like ceiling height, natural light, and sleeping alcove configuration create wide price variation.

### How much space do you actually get in a studio?

Studios typically range from 400 to 600 square feet nationally (as of Q3 2026). Compact studios in expensive cities like San Francisco or New York may start around 300 square feet. Alcove studios, which include a recessed sleeping area, tend to fall between 500 and 650 square feet. Usable space depends heavily on layout efficiency, not just total square footage.

### What furniture fits in a studio apartment?

Most studios accommodate a bed (queen or full, rarely king), a two-seat sofa or loveseat, a small dining table for two, a desk, and a dresser. Storage furniture like bookcases or wardrobes becomes necessary if the unit lacks closet space. Multipurpose furniture, such as a bed with storage drawers, helps maximize floor area in studios under 500 square feet.

### Should I get a studio to save money for a down payment?

A studio can accelerate savings meaningfully. In a market where the studio vs 1 bedroom rent gap is $500 per month, choosing the studio saves $6,000 per year, plus an additional $600 to $1,200 in utility savings. Over a two-year lease, that is $12,000 to $13,200 redirected toward a savings goal. The tradeoff is daily comfort, so weigh the timeline against your tolerance.

### How long should I stay in a studio before upgrading?

Most renters who start in studios transition to one-bedrooms within 18 to 24 months. Life changes, including new relationships, remote work shifts, and accumulated belongings, tend to push renters toward more space over time. Signing a shorter initial lease, such as [month-to-month](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/month-to-month-vs-12-month-lease), gives flexibility to move when your situation changes without paying a [lease-break penalty](https://www.brightplace.ai/resources/short-term-lease-agreement).
